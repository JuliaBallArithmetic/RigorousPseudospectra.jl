\begin{abstract}
We give a matrix-free, interval-rigorous method that encloses the leading eigenvalues of a
large non-normal operator $A$ --- available only through the maps $x\mapsto Ax$, $x\mapsto A^*x$
--- and rigorously bounds its smallest singular value $\sigmin(A-zI)$ (equivalently the resolvent
norm) on curves in the complex plane. The method assembles from two components, each of independent
interest, joined by a third. The first is the \emph{Miyajima--Rump} verified block diagonalization
for dense matrices, which certifies eigenvalue enclosures and resolvent bounds from a single Neumann
residual, with the non-normality confined to small diagonal blocks and only a benign inter-block
conditioning transported through $\kappa_2$. The second is a \emph{certified Lanczos decomposition}:
a tridiagonal Lanczos with partial reorthogonalization whose loss of orthogonality and deviation are
bounded rigorously --- streamed from the scalar recurrence coefficients \`a la Paige --- yielding a
verified two-sided enclosure of the extremal eigenvalues of any Hermitian operator supplied only as a
matvec. We stress that this floor requires the \emph{full} basis: Ritz values of a partial Krylov space
bound $\lambda_{\min}$ only from above, and it is squareness of the basis that converts the residual
identity into a similarity and so supplies the missing completeness. A certified method is
correspondingly more expensive than a floating-point one --- guaranteeing that no direction was missed
is a statement about the whole space --- but the verified overhead beyond the matvecs is $O(1)$ per step. Joining the two gives the third: a certified Krylov
method. A Krylov reduction splits $A$ into a dense \emph{captured} block, enclosed by Miyajima--Rump,
and a matrix-free \emph{far} block, whose smallest singular value is floored by the certified Lanczos.
The central observation making the join work is that for a non-normal operator the eigenvector
(Arnoldi) Krylov space does \emph{not} span the leading singular directions --- so deflating it leaves
the far block shallow in operator norm --- and that augmenting the captured subspace with the top right
singular vectors, obtained from a Lanczos process on $A^*A$, makes the far block \emph{deep}: the
certified-Lanczos floor then converges with a well-separated bottom. The captured compression is
\emph{faithful} ($\sigmin(G_{11}-zI)\approx\sigmin(A-zI)$), so its verified SVD is $\kappa_2$-free.
The central conclusion is a \emph{pseudospectral inclusion}: once the deflation has made the far block
deep relative to a chosen radius $R$, the $\delta$-pseudospectrum of $A$ outside $R$ is contained in
the $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum of the small captured block, with an explicit relation between
$\varepsilon$ and $\delta$. A single inequality, valid at every $z$ simultaneously, thus transfers the
entire exterior spectral behaviour of a large matrix-free operator onto a small dense matrix one can
factor; in the limit $\varepsilon\to0$ it says that the eigenvalues found outside $R$ are \emph{all} of
them, with no grid, no covering argument and no a-priori bound on the spectral radius. Circles are
rigorously covered by an adaptive pearl cover, and each eigenvalue is rigorously enclosed by a cheap
probe that locates the scale followed by a single verified check. We report rigorous
enclosures for the Perron--Frobenius (Ulam) operators of the noisy H\'enon and contracting-Lorenz maps
and the Gauss--Kuzmin--Wirsing operator, and we map the method's applicability on a gallery of standard
non-normal matrices: it succeeds precisely when the singular spectrum decays (a low-dimensional
resolvent tail) and \emph{fails safely} --- a non-positive floor or an explicit refusal, never a false
enclosure --- when it does not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Let $A\in\C^{n\times n}$, or more generally a linear operator on $\C^n$ available only through the
two maps $x\mapsto Ax$ and $x\mapsto A^*x$. We want \emph{rigorous} answers to two coupled questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item enclose the leading eigenvalues of $A$ in rigorous discs, each disc carrying a rigorous
      eigenvalue count; and
\item bound $\sigmin(A-zI)=1/\norm{(A-zI)^{-1}}_2$ from below on a curve $\Gamma\subset\C$ --- a
      rigorous resolvent bound, hence a rigorous statement about the $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum.
\end{enumerate}
``Rigorous'' means every reported number is an outward-rounded interval (ball) enclosure: the
floating-point computation produces \emph{candidates}, and a second phase, carried out in
midpoint--radius ball arithmetic, bounds the residual of those candidates. We use the ball-arithmetic
kernel of \texttt{BallArithmetic.jl}; the numerics below are reproducible in the companion package.

The difficulty is \emph{non-normality}. For a normal operator the eigenvalues, the singular values,
and the resolvent norm are all governed by the same orthonormal frame, and a Krylov compression is
benign. Transfer operators of chaotic maps --- our motivating application --- are the opposite: they
are strongly non-normal, their spectrum is a cloud strictly inside the unit disk, and
$\norm{(A-zI)^{-1}}_2$ can be enormous at points far from the spectrum~\cite{TrefethenEmbree2005}. Two symptoms drive the paper:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(S1)] \textbf{Arnoldi Ritz values can mislead.} The classic example is the cyclic shift, whose
      eigenvalues are the roots of unity but whose Arnoldi Ritz values from a coordinate start are
      pinned near the origin (Section~\ref{sec:examples}). A rigorous method must never trust a Ritz
      value; it must certify.
\item[(S2)] \textbf{The eigenvector Krylov space misses the singular directions.} We measure, on a
      $256$-dimensional Krylov block of a discretized noisy-H\'enon operator, that
      $\sigmax(H)=1.48<\sigmax(A)=1.71$: the compression $H=V^*AV$ does not even contain the leading
      \emph{singular} direction of $A$. Consequently, deflating the Krylov space barely reduces the
      operator norm of the complementary block ($0.43$ against the true $\sigma_{257}(A)=0.12$), and
      a matrix-free $\sigmin$ certificate on that shallow far block does not converge.
\end{itemize}
Our contribution is organized as three components. \textbf{(i)} We recall the Miyajima--Rump verified
block diagonalization (Sections~\ref{sec:mr}--\ref{sec:mrres}), which encloses the eigenvalues of a
dense block and bounds its resolvent from a single Neumann residual. \textbf{(ii)} We isolate a
\emph{certified Lanczos decomposition} (Section~\ref{sec:lanczos}) --- a tridiagonal, partially
reorthogonalized Lanczos whose orthogonality loss and deviation are bounded rigorously from the
scalar coefficients alone --- as a self-contained tool that encloses the extremal eigenvalues of any
Hermitian matvec operator; it is useful independently of what follows, and it comes with a sharp
delimitation of what a Krylov space can and cannot certify (Proposition~\ref{prop:lanczosfloor}).
The one hypothesis of (ii) --- certified extremal eigenvalues of the small tridiagonal $T_N$ --- is
discharged without any verified eigensolver at all, by a rigorous Cholesky/inertia definiteness test
costing $O(N)$ (Remark~\ref{rem:sturm}).

The payoff of the join, and the main theorem of the paper, is Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}: for a
radius $R$ exceeding the depth $\norm{G_{22}}_2$ of the far block,
\[
  \Lambda_{\delta(\varepsilon)}(A)\cap\{\abs{z}>R\}\;\subseteq\;\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(G_{11})\cap\{\abs{z}>R\},
  \qquad
  \delta(\varepsilon)=\frac{\varepsilon\,(R-\norm{G_{22}}_2)}{\varepsilon+(R-\norm{G_{22}}_2)+\gamma},
\]
so the whole exterior pseudospectral structure of the large operator is controlled by that of a small
dense block. This is the sense in which the method is a \emph{pseudospectral} rather than merely an
eigenvalue algorithm. A homotopy in the coupling then upgrades the inclusion to an exact count
(Proposition~\ref{prop:count}): $A$ and $G_{11}$ have the \emph{same number} of eigenvalues outside
$R$, so the certified list is complete in both directions --- nothing of $A$ is missed, and nothing
listed is a projection artifact --- rather than being whatever the iteration happened to find. \textbf{(iii)} We \emph{join} the two
(Sections~\ref{sec:krylov}--\ref{sec:krylovcert}): a Krylov reduction certifies the eigenvalue
\emph{block} with (i) and floors the matrix-free \emph{far} block with (ii), the far block made deep
by deflating the top \emph{singular} directions of $A$ --- obtained by a Lanczos on $A^*A$ --- in
addition to the eigenvector directions. The resulting certificate is matrix-free, sound, and certifies
the leading spectrum in tens of seconds --- \emph{when the singular values of $A$ decay}, so that the
far block can be made deep with a modest deflation. This is the transfer-operator regime; on full-rank
non-normal matrices the method fails safely rather than universally succeeding
(Section~\ref{sec:testmat}), which is the honest scope of the approach and, we argue, a necessary one.

\paragraph{A note on scope.} When $A$ is the Ulam (Galerkin) discretization of an infinite-dimensional
transfer operator, everything below is a rigorous statement about the \emph{matrix} $A$. Lifting it to
the underlying operator requires, in addition, a rigorous bound on the discretization
(e.g.\ $\mathrm{BV}\!\to\!L^1$) error; that ingredient is orthogonal to this paper and is not included.

\section{Miyajima--Rump enclosure for dense matrices}\label{sec:mr}

We first recall the verified block diagonalization for a dense $B\in\C^{m\times m}$~\cite{Rump2022,Miyajima2014}. It is the engine
we will run on the captured Krylov block; we state it for a general $B$.

\paragraph{Two-phase pattern.} A floating-point eigensolver returns a candidate similarity: an
approximate frame $W$ and its approximate inverse $Y\approx W^{-1}$ (never an exact inverse), chosen
so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Atilde}
  \widetilde B \;=\; Y\,B\,W \;\approx\; \Lambda \;=\; \operatorname{blockdiag}(P_1,\dots,P_q),
\end{equation}
a block-diagonal candidate. The columns of $W$ are grouped into clusters $\mathcal C_1,\dots,\mathcal C_q$;
$P_j=\widetilde B[\mathcal C_j,\mathcal C_j]$. The candidate phase is Float64; the certification phase,
below, is ball arithmetic.

\paragraph{Block-orthonormal frame (VBD).} Forcing $W$ to be the eigenvector matrix is dangerous: for
a near-defective cluster the eigenbasis is ill-conditioned or does not exist, inflating $\kappa_2(W)$.
Instead we build $W$ from the \emph{Schur} vectors and re-orthonormalize \emph{within} each cluster
but \emph{not} across clusters. The frame is then \emph{block-orthonormal}: $W_{\mathcal C_j}$ has
orthonormal columns, so $\kappa_2(W)$ is governed by the \emph{inter-block} subspace angles
($\approx 1/\sin\theta_{\mathrm{inter}}$, benign and separation-controlled), while the intra-cluster
non-normality is retained inside $P_j$. A Newton (Sylvester) step reduces the off-block part of
$\widetilde B$ toward the $\kappa\,u$ floor. We call the result the \emph{verified block
diagonalization} (VBD).

\paragraph{The single residual.} Two ball quantities certify everything:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residuals}
  R_2 \;=\; Y W - I, \qquad \widetilde B = Y B W,
\end{equation}
computed as ball products with outward rounding. If $\norm{R_2}_2<1$ (verified via a rigorous
$\ell^2$ operator-norm bound), then $YW=I+R_2$ is invertible with
$\norm{(YW)^{-1}}_2\le 1/(1-\norm{R_2}_2)$ (Neumann), so $W$ is nonsingular, $Y$ is a genuine
left inverse up to $R_2$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa}
  \kappa_2(W) \;\le\; \frac{\norm{Y}_2}{1-\norm{R_2}_2}\cdot\norm{W}_2
  \qquad(\text{no \texttt{cond}, no exact inverse}).
\end{equation}
The deviation of the collapsed $M=W^{-1}BW$ from the candidate $\Lambda$ is bounded by one slack:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:blockdev}
  \norm{M-\Lambda}_2 \;\le\; \beta_\Lambda
  \;:=\; \norm{N}_2 + \frac{\norm{R_2}_2\,\norm{\widetilde B}_2}{1-\norm{R_2}_2},
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the off-block part of $\widetilde B$ (the inter-block leakage). Crucially $\beta_\Lambda$
\emph{subsumes} the coupling: there is no separate inter-block term.

\begin{theorem}[Spectral enclosure]\label{thm:enclosure}
Suppose $\norm{R_2}_2<1$. Then every eigenvalue of $B$ lies in
$\bigcup_j\Omega_j$ with $\Omega_j=\{z:\sigmin(P_j-zI)\le \beta_\Lambda\}$, and each connected
component of $k$ overlapping regions $\Omega_j$ contains exactly $\sum$ of the corresponding block
sizes eigenvalues of $B$. For a singleton block $P_j=[\widetilde B_{jj}]$ the region is the disc
$\abs{z-\widetilde B_{jj}}\le\beta_\Lambda$; for a $k$-fold cluster it is the block region, which
tracks $\abs{z-\lambda}^k$ near a defective $\lambda$.
\end{theorem}

The eigenvalue count follows from a homotopy/argument-principle argument on the block-diagonal
candidate: $\Lambda-zI$ and $M-zI$ have the same number of eigenvalues inside a contour on which
$\sigmin(\Lambda-zI)>\beta_\Lambda$, because $\norm{M-\Lambda}_2\le\beta_\Lambda$ keeps them from
crossing. The clustering (which blocks share a component) is decided by disc overlap and is
\emph{self-correcting}: an over-optimistic split whose rigorous regions still overlap is re-merged.

\begin{remark}[$\kappa_2$ is a diagnostic, not part of the enclosure]
The enclosure of Theorem~\ref{thm:enclosure} does not use $\kappa_2$: it reads $\sigmin(P_j-zI)$ off
the diagonal blocks and subtracts one slack. The conditioning $\kappa_2(W)$ enters only the
resolvent transport of Section~\ref{sec:mrres}. We compute it cheaply (a Collatz $\ell^2$ bound) by
default, or tightly (one verified SVD of $W$) on request.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What is Rump's, and what is ours]\label{rem:rump}
Rump's verified all-eigenpairs method~\cite{Rump2022} (and Miyajima's earlier
enclosures~\cite{Miyajima2014}) certify \emph{discrete} spectral data: the eigenvalues, and a basis of
each invariant subspace, of a dense matrix in $O(n^3)$, from the almost-diagonal $\widetilde B=YBW=D+E$
and a single contraction condition ($\varrho(\mathbf Z)<1$ in Rump's notation, our $\norm{R_2}_2<1$).
There is no $z$-parameterized quantity in that work: no resolvent norm, no $\sigmin(A-zI)$, no
pseudospectrum, and no Schur-complement or Krylov/matrix-free ingredient. Everything from
Section~\ref{sec:mrres} onward --- the transport to a \emph{resolvent} lower bound
$\sigmin(B-zI)$ over a \emph{region} of $z$, its block-Ger\v sgorin localization, the Schur-complement
block assembly, and the matrix-free Lanczos far block --- is the pseudospectral layer this paper adds
on top of that enclosure. We use the block-orthonormal \emph{Schur}+Newton frame (rather than the raw
eigenvector matrix) precisely so that this resolvent layer inherits a benign $\kappa_2$.
\end{remark}

\section{Resolvent bounds from Miyajima--Rump}\label{sec:mrres}

The same data bound the resolvent, cheaply, away from a small exclusion set. Since
$B-zI=W(M-zI)W^{-1}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transport}
  \sigmin(B-zI)\;\ge\;\frac{\sigmin(M-zI)}{\kappa_2(W)}.
\end{equation}
Block-diagonality gives $\sigmin(\Lambda-zI)=\min_j\sigmin(P_j-zI)$, and $\sigmin$ is $1$-Lipschitz,
so $\sigmin(M-zI)\ge\min_j\sigmin(P_j-zI)-\beta_\Lambda$. With \eqref{eq:transport} this is the
primary bound.

\begin{lemma}[Block resolvent floor]\label{lem:blockres}
With $\beta_\Lambda$ from \eqref{eq:blockdev} and $\kappa_2(W)$ from \eqref{eq:kappa},
\[
  \sigmin(B-zI)\;\ge\;\frac{\max\!\big(0,\ \min_j\sigmin(P_j-zI)-\beta_\Lambda\big)}{\kappa_2(W)}.
\]
Per $z$ the cost is one small $\sigmin(P_j-zI)$ per block (closed form for $\abs{\mathcal C_j}\le2$,
a verified SVD otherwise); a singleton contributes only the far field $\abs{z-\widetilde B_{jj}}$.
The non-normality of a defective cluster lives in $\sigmin(P_j-zI)$ (it tracks $\abs{z-\lambda}^m$),
\emph{not} in $\kappa_2(W)$, which carries only the benign inter-block conditioning.
\end{lemma}

A localized variant charges each block only its own coupling radius. With
$r_j=\norm{N[\mathcal C_j,:]}_2$ the block-row norm of the off-block part and
$s_j(z)=\sigmin(P_j-zI)$, if $\tau(z)^2=\sum_j (r_j/s_j(z))^2<1$ then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:blockdom}
  \sigmin(B-zI)\;\ge\;\frac{(\min_j s_j(z))(1-\tau(z))}{\kappa_2(W)}
\end{equation}
(factor $M-zI=(\Lambda-zI)(I+(\Lambda-zI)^{-1}(M-\Lambda))$ and bound the block rows). The engine
returns the maximum of Lemma~\ref{lem:blockres} and \eqref{eq:blockdom}.

\paragraph{The exact-SVD escape.} Both bounds read $\sigmin$ off the block-diagonal \emph{surrogate}
and pay $\kappa_2(W)$. When $B$ is small enough to factor a verified SVD directly, $\sigmin(B-zI)$ is instead enclosed \emph{exactly} by a single $\svdbox(B-zI)$~\cite{Miyajima2014SV} per $z$, with \emph{no} $\kappa_2$
factor: the SVD sees the true non-normality of the whole block. For a moderate captured block this
costs $O(m^3)$ per sample (milliseconds) and recovers exactly the $\kappa_2$ the surrogate concedes;
it is our default in Section~\ref{sec:krylovcert}. The block-diagonal layer is reserved for blocks too
large for one SVD, where its $O(\sum_j m_j^3)\ll O(m^3)$ cost is worth the $\kappa_2$ price. There is
\emph{no} per-block $\kappa_2$: the transport \eqref{eq:transport} is global; the escape is the exact
SVD, not a local conditioning constant.

\section{A certified Lanczos decomposition}\label{sec:lanczos}

The second component stands on its own, independent of the eigenvalue problem above. Stated plainly,
it is a \emph{certified extremal singular-value solver for sparse or matrix-free Hermitian operators}:
given $M=M^*$ available only as a matvec $x\mapsto Mx$, it returns rigorous \emph{two-sided} bounds on
$\lambda_{\min}(M)$ and $\lambda_{\max}(M)$ --- equivalently, for $M=C^*C$, on $\sigmin(C)$ and
$\sigmax(C)$. It is the verified counterpart of the symmetric Lanczos iteration, and it is reusable
wherever such a bound is wanted; nothing in this section refers to $A$.

Both ends are needed downstream, and for different reasons. Section~\ref{sec:krylovcert} applies the
$\lambda_{\max}$ bound to $G_{22}^*G_{22}$ to certify $\norm{G_{22}}_2$ --- the single hypothesis
\eqref{eq:onehypothesis} on which the whole exterior certificate rests --- and the $\lambda_{\min}$
bound to $(G_{22}-zI)^*(G_{22}-zI)$ to floor $\sig_{22}$ where the $z$-independent shortcut does not
apply. A one-sided tool would not suffice, and, as we will see, the two ends are not of differing
difficulty: both require the same completeness certificate, because a missed \emph{large} eigenvalue
is exactly as invisible to a partial Krylov space as a missed small one.

\paragraph{The finite-precision recurrence.} A Lanczos process on $M$ builds a tridiagonal
$T_k=\operatorname{tridiag}(\bar\beta_{j-1},\bar\alpha_j,\bar\beta_j)$ and a nominally orthonormal
basis $\bar Q_k=[\bar q_1,\dots,\bar q_k]$. In floating point the computed vectors satisfy the
\emph{perturbed} three-term recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lanczosrec}
  \bar\beta_j\,\bar q_{j+1}\;=\;M\bar q_j-\bar\alpha_j\bar q_j-\bar\beta_{j-1}\bar q_{j-1}+f_j,
\end{equation}
with local error $f_j$, and they \emph{lose orthogonality} as small Ritz values converge. We run
\emph{partial (selective) reorthogonalization}: orthogonality is monitored and repaired only against
the converged Ritz directions, keeping the cost near that of plain Lanczos while bounding the drift.
Two residual matrices carry all the rigor,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lanczosres}
  R_2\;=\;\bar Q_k^*\bar Q_k-I \quad(\text{loss of orthogonality}),\qquad
  R_1\;=\;\bar Q_k^*\big(M\bar Q_k-\bar Q_kT_k\big)\quad(\text{Ritz residual}).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Streaming the bound (Paige).} The decisive point is that neither residual needs the stored
basis or any inner product: Paige's rounding-error analysis of \eqref{eq:lanczosrec} \emph{streams} a
rigorous entrywise bound on $R_2$ (and on $R_1$) from the \emph{scalar} coefficients
$(\bar\alpha_j,\bar\beta_j,\norm{f_j})$ alone~\cite{Paige1976,Rump2010}. We accumulate a nonnegative
matrix $W\ge\abs{R_2}$ entrywise as the iteration proceeds and read off
$\norm{R_2}_2\le\sqrt{\norm{W}_1\norm{W}_\infty}$; likewise $\norm{R_1}_2$. The basis itself may be
discarded --- only the tridiagonal $T_k$ and a handful of running scalars are kept --- so the memory is
$O(k)$ and the verified overhead is negligible against the matvecs.

\paragraph{Why a partial basis cannot give a floor.} It is tempting to read a lower bound off the
Ritz values after $k\ll N$ steps. It is not available, and the obstruction is structural rather than
one of sharpness. Since $T_k=\bar Q_k^*M\bar Q_k$ is the \emph{compression} of $M$ to
$\mathcal S=\operatorname{ran}\bar Q_k$, the min--max principle gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ritzabove}
  \theta_{\min}\;=\;\min_{0\ne x\in\mathcal S}\frac{x^*Mx}{x^*x}\;\ge\;\lambda_{\min}(M)
  \qquad\text{for every }k:
\end{equation}
Ritz values approach $\lambda_{\min}$ \emph{from above}. The deficit
$\theta_{\min}-\lambda_{\min}(M)$ is governed by the angle $\angle(u_{\min},\mathcal S)$ between the
true minimizing eigenvector and the Krylov space; if $u_{\min}\perp\mathcal S$ the deficit is the whole
spectral gap, and \emph{no residual can detect this}. Indeed the projected residual
$R_1=\bar Q_k^*S_k$ is small \emph{by construction} for every $k$, because
$S_k=(I-\bar Q_k\bar Q_k^*)M\bar Q_k$ is orthogonal to $\mathcal S$: projecting it back onto
$\mathcal S$ annihilates precisely the evidence of incompleteness. What the residual \emph{does}
certify is the reverse inclusion --- each Ritz pair is an approximate eigenpair, so
$\spec(T_k)\subseteq\spec(M)+[-\beta,\beta]$, giving an \emph{upper} bound on $\lambda_{\min}(M)$.
A floor needs the \emph{completeness} direction $\spec(M)\subseteq\spec(T_k)+[-\beta,\beta]$, and for
that the basis must be complete.

\begin{proposition}[Certified Lanczos floor, full basis]\label{prop:lanczosfloor}
Let $M=M^*$ act on an invariant subspace $\mathcal V$ of dimension $N$ containing the start vector, and
run the Lanczos above for $k=N$ steps (restarting at breakdowns as in Remark~\ref{rem:breakdown}),
producing the computed basis $\bar Q_N=[\bar q_1,\dots,\bar q_N]$, the symmetric (block-)tridiagonal
$T_N$, the local defects $f_j$ of \eqref{eq:lanczosrec}, and the trailing $\bar\beta_N$. Put
\[
  \rho\;=\;\norm{R_2}_2=\norm{\bar Q_N^*\bar Q_N-I}_2,\qquad
  \eta\;=\;\bar\beta_N\norm{\bar q_{N+1}}_2+\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^N\norm{f_j}^2\Big)^{1/2},\qquad
  \beta_{\mathrm L}\;=\;\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{1-\rho}} ,
\]
and let $\theta_{\min}^-\le\lambda_{\min}(T_N)$ and $\theta_{\max}^+\ge\lambda_{\max}(T_N)$ be certified
bounds for the tridiagonal (Remark~\ref{rem:sturm}). If $\rho<1$ then $\bar Q_N$ is invertible and
\[
  \spec\!\big(M|_{\mathcal V}\big)\;\subseteq\;\spec(T_N)+[-\beta_{\mathrm L},\,\beta_{\mathrm L}],
\]
so in particular
\[
  \lambda_{\min}\!\big(M|_{\mathcal V}\big)\;\ge\;\theta_{\min}^--\beta_{\mathrm L},
  \qquad
  \lambda_{\max}\!\big(M|_{\mathcal V}\big)\;\le\;\theta_{\max}^++\beta_{\mathrm L},
\]
and for $M=(G-zI)^*(G-zI)$, $\ \sigmin(G-zI)\ge\sqrt{\max(0,\,\theta_{\min}^--\beta_{\mathrm L})}$.
All of $\rho$, $\bar\beta_N$, $\norm{f_j}$ are streamed from the scalar recurrence.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: $\bar Q_N$ is invertible, with a quantitative bound.} By definition
$\bar Q_N^*\bar Q_N=I+R_2$, a Hermitian matrix, so by Weyl's inequality
$\lambda_{\min}(\bar Q_N^*\bar Q_N)\ge1-\norm{R_2}_2=1-\rho>0$. Since
$\sigmin(\bar Q_N)^2=\lambda_{\min}(\bar Q_N^*\bar Q_N)$, the matrix $\bar Q_N$ is nonsingular and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qinv}
  \norm{\bar Q_N^{-1}}_2\;=\;\frac{1}{\sigmin(\bar Q_N)}\;\le\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho}} .
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 2: the residual, columnwise.} Write $S:=M\bar Q_N-\bar Q_NT_N$. Rearranging
\eqref{eq:lanczosrec} at step $j$ gives the identity actually realized in floating point,
\[
  M\bar q_j\;=\;\bar\beta_{j-1}\bar q_{j-1}+\bar\alpha_j\bar q_j+\bar\beta_j\bar q_{j+1}-f_j .
\]
For $j<N$ the $j$-th column of $\bar Q_NT_N$ is exactly
$\bar\beta_{j-1}\bar q_{j-1}+\bar\alpha_j\bar q_j+\bar\beta_j\bar q_{j+1}$, so $S e_j=-f_j$. For $j=N$
the vector $\bar q_{N+1}$ is not a column of $\bar Q_N$ and $T_N$ carries no $(N+1,N)$ entry, so the
term $\bar\beta_N\bar q_{N+1}$ survives: $Se_N=\bar\beta_N\bar q_{N+1}-f_N$. Hence
\[
  S\;=\;-F_N+\bar\beta_N\,\bar q_{N+1}e_N^{*},\qquad F_N=[f_1,\dots,f_N],
\]
and therefore, using $\norm{F_N}_2\le\norm{F_N}_F$ and $\norm{\bar q_{N+1}e_N^*}_2=\norm{\bar q_{N+1}}_2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sbound}
  \norm{S}_2\;\le\;\Big(\textstyle\sum_j\norm{f_j}^2\Big)^{1/2}+\bar\beta_N\norm{\bar q_{N+1}}_2\;=\;\eta .
\end{equation}
(At a restart boundary $j=k$ one sets $\bar\beta_k=0$ in $T_N$, so the $k$-th column of $\bar Q_NT_N$
is $\bar\beta_{k-1}\bar q_{k-1}+\bar\alpha_k\bar q_k$ and $Se_k$ is the measured breakdown residual,
which is recorded in $f_k$; the form \eqref{eq:Sbound} is unchanged.)

\emph{Step 3: squareness turns the residual into a similarity.} This is the only place the hypothesis
$k=N$ is used, and it is essential. Because $\bar Q_N$ is \emph{square} and, by Step 1, invertible,
$M\bar Q_N=\bar Q_NT_N+S$ may be multiplied on the left by $\bar Q_N^{-1}$:
\[
  \bar Q_N^{-1}M\bar Q_N\;=\;T_N+E,\qquad E:=\bar Q_N^{-1}S,
\]
whence, by \eqref{eq:Qinv} and \eqref{eq:Sbound},
$\norm{E}_2\le\norm{\bar Q_N^{-1}}_2\norm{S}_2\le\eta/\sqrt{1-\rho}=\beta_{\mathrm L}$. Since
$\operatorname{ran}\bar Q_N=\mathcal V$ and similarity preserves the spectrum,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:specequal}
  \spec\!\big(M|_{\mathcal V}\big)\;=\;\spec(T_N+E)\qquad\text{\emph{exactly}} .
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 4: Bauer--Fike.} Let $\mu\in\spec(T_N+E)$ and suppose $\mu\notin\spec(T_N)$. Then
$T_N-\mu I$ is invertible and
\[
  T_N+E-\mu I\;=\;(T_N-\mu I)\big(I+(T_N-\mu I)^{-1}E\big)
\]
is singular, so $I+(T_N-\mu I)^{-1}E$ is singular, forcing
$\norm{(T_N-\mu I)^{-1}E}_2\ge1$ and hence $\norm{(T_N-\mu I)^{-1}}_2\norm{E}_2\ge1$. The matrix $T_N$
is real symmetric, hence normal, so $\norm{(T_N-\mu I)^{-1}}_2=1/\operatorname{dist}(\mu,\spec(T_N))$;
combining, $\operatorname{dist}(\mu,\spec(T_N))\le\norm{E}_2\le\beta_{\mathrm L}$. The same holds
trivially when $\mu\in\spec(T_N)$. With \eqref{eq:specequal} this is exactly the asserted inclusion
$\spec(M|_{\mathcal V})\subseteq\spec(T_N)+[-\beta_{\mathrm L},\beta_{\mathrm L}]$.

\emph{Step 5: the extremal bounds.} By the inclusion, $\lambda_{\min}(M|_{\mathcal V})$ lies within
$\beta_{\mathrm L}$ of some $\theta_i\in\spec(T_N)$, so
$\lambda_{\min}(M|_{\mathcal V})\ge\theta_i-\beta_{\mathrm L}\ge\lambda_{\min}(T_N)-\beta_{\mathrm L}
\ge\theta_{\min}^--\beta_{\mathrm L}$; symmetrically for $\lambda_{\max}$. Finally, for
$M=(G-zI)^*(G-zI)\succeq0$ one has $\sigmin(G-zI)=\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(M)}$, and the square root is
monotone, giving the last claim after truncating a negative right-hand side at $0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Certifying $\theta_{\min}$: a Cholesky test, in $O(N)$]\label{rem:sturm}
The proposition needs a rigorous \emph{lower} bound $\theta_{\min}^-$ on $\lambda_{\min}(T_N)$, not the
floating-point output of a tridiagonal eigensolver. The right tool is Rump's positive-definiteness
certificate~\cite{Rump2006}: a one-sided eigenvalue bound is equivalent to a definiteness statement,
\[
  \lambda_{\min}(T_N)>s\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T_N-sI\succ0
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the Cholesky (}LDL^\top\text{) factorization of }T_N-sI\text{ exists,}
\]
so it suffices to \emph{certify a factorization}, never to compute a spectrum. The remarkable feature
of Rump's formulation is that the certificate is the \emph{success of the ordinary, library-optimized
floating-point factorization}. If the float64 Cholesky of $C=T_N-sI$ runs to completion, then by the
classical backward analysis there exists $\Delta C$ with
\[
  C+\Delta C=R^\top R\ \succeq\ 0,\qquad \abs{\Delta C}\le\gamma_{N+1}\abs{R^\top}\abs{R}\ \ \text{entrywise},
  \qquad \gamma_k=\frac{ku}{1-ku},
\]
so $C\succeq-\Delta C$ and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rumpchol}
  \lambda_{\min}(T_N)\;\ge\;s-\norm{\Delta C}_2,\qquad
  \norm{\Delta C}_2\le\gamma_{N+1}\big\|\abs{R^\top}\abs{R}\big\|_1 .
\end{equation}
No interval arithmetic enters the inner loop: one runs the vendor kernel at full speed and computes a
single a-posteriori norm. (The shift is essential --- an \emph{unshifted} successful Cholesky yields
only $\lambda_{\min}\ge-\norm{\Delta C}$, which is vacuous; shifting first converts it into a genuine
positive bound.)

For a symmetric tridiagonal this is as cheap as it can possibly be, \emph{provided the structure is
preserved}. The $LDL^\top$ pivots satisfy the Sturm recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sturm}
  d_1=\alpha_1-s,\qquad d_i=(\alpha_i-s)-\frac{\beta_{i-1}^2}{d_{i-1}},
\end{equation}
and $T_N-sI\succ0$ exactly when all $d_i>0$; more generally $\#\{i:d_i<0\}$ counts the eigenvalues
below $s$ (Sylvester's law of inertia), so the same sweep certifies $\theta_{\max}^+$ from a count of
$N$. Both the factorization and the norm in \eqref{eq:rumpchol} are then $O(N)$: with $R=L\sqrt D$
lower bidiagonal, $\abs{R^\top}\abs{R}$ is tridiagonal and its $1$-norm is a single sweep. A dense
Cholesky call on the same matrix would allocate $O(N^2)$ and is not an option at these sizes --- in our
implementation the tridiagonal factorization of an $N=10^6$ operator certifies a bound in $0.03$\,s
using $38$\,MB, where a densified call would require $7.5$\,TB. One may equally run
\eqref{eq:sturm} directly in outward-rounded ball arithmetic, each pivot then carrying its own radius;
the two routes agree to full working precision in our tests.

It is worth saying why we do \emph{not} take the tidier-looking route. $T_N$ is Hermitian and dense
methods apply to it, so the Miyajima--Rump enclosure of Section~\ref{sec:mr} would certify all of its
eigenvalues, and would do so in the most benign possible setting ($T_N$ real symmetric $\Rightarrow$
orthogonal frame $\Rightarrow\kappa_2(W)=1$, transport \eqref{eq:transport} free). That would be a
pleasing closure --- the first pillar of the paper discharging the hypothesis of the second. But it
runs \texttt{eigen} and an approximate inverse on a dense $N\times N$: $O(N^3)$ time and $O(N^2)$
memory. For a far block with $N$ in the thousands that dominates the $N$ matvecs and throws away the
structure, so it is appropriate only for small $N$. The definiteness test is not a fallback but the
correct algorithm: certifying one number requires certifying a factorization, not a diagonalization.

The bound \eqref{eq:rumpchol} is rigorous by construction; what is worth quantifying is its
\emph{sharpness}, since a certificate is of little use if it concedes orders of magnitude. It concedes
almost nothing here. The slack is $\gamma_{N+1}\norm{\abs{R^\top}\abs{R}}_1=O(Nu\norm{T_N})$, so
relative to $\norm{T_N}$ it is at the level of the working precision; on random tridiagonals we measure
a relative slack below $5\times10^{-15}$, and the returned bracket agrees with the floating-point
$\lambda_{\min}$ to full double precision. Consequently the uncertainty this step contributes to
$\theta_{\min}^-$ is dominated by $\beta_{\mathrm L}$ throughout Section~\ref{sec:examples}. The
symmetric eigenproblem is moreover perfectly conditioned (Weyl), so no $\kappa$ is incurred anywhere
in this step.
\end{remark}

Three points deserve emphasis. First, the residual enters \emph{bare}: it is $\bar\beta_N$ itself, not
its projection $\bar Q_N^*S$, that measures the deviation --- and this is what makes the bound
self-policing. At $k<N$ the same formula has $\bar\beta_k=O(\norm{T})$, so $\beta_{\mathrm L}$ swamps
$\theta_{\min}$ and the floor returns a non-positive (vacuous but sound) value rather than a
number one might mistake for a certificate. Second, the test $\rho<1$ now plays a single honest role
--- it certifies that the computed basis is genuinely a \emph{basis} --- while completeness comes from
the dimension count $k=N$, not from any residual. Third, the bound is \emph{tight} at $k=N$: the
Krylov space is exhausted, so $\bar\beta_N$ falls to rounding level.

\begin{remark}[Breakdown is not completeness]\label{rem:breakdown}
If $\bar\beta_k\approx0$ at some $k<N$ the Krylov space has closed on itself: $\operatorname{ran}\bar Q_k$
is (nearly) invariant and $\spec(T_k)$ is a near-\emph{subset} of $\spec(M)$ --- again the wrong
direction, since $\lambda_{\min}(M)$ may lie entirely on the untouched orthogonal complement. A
``happy breakdown'' recovers the full spectrum only under a genericity assumption on the start vector
(that it has a nonzero component in every eigenspace), which we do not certify --- and which finite
precision makes delicate, a $10^{-18}$ component being indistinguishable from none. We therefore do not
stop at breakdown: we set the block boundary $\bar\beta_k=0$ and restart from a fresh vector
orthogonal to the accumulated basis, so the basis still attains dimension $N$. Then $T_N$ is
block-tridiagonal, its spectrum is the union of the blocks', and Proposition~\ref{prop:lanczosfloor}
applies verbatim.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The cost of rigor]\label{rem:cost}
Proposition~\ref{prop:lanczosfloor} requires $N$ matvecs, where a non-rigorous estimate would stop
after a few dozen. This is not an artifact of our analysis but the price of the guarantee: certifying
that \emph{no} direction --- in particular the minimizing one --- has been missed is a statement about
the whole space, and a partial Krylov space simply does not contain that information. The certified
method is necessarily more expensive than the floating-point one; what remains cheap is everything
\emph{else}, since $\rho$, $\bar\beta_N$ and the $\norm{f_j}$ are streamed from the scalar recurrence
at $O(1)$ overhead per step. In practice the far block is where this cost is paid, and it is paid once
per operator, not per $z$ (Section~\ref{sec:farblock}).
\end{remark}

The floor is tightest when the wanted end of the spectrum is \emph{well separated} from the bulk, since
then the $\bar\beta_j$ stay healthy and $\rho$ remains small; a nearly-multiple bottom eigenvalue
inflates $\rho$ and the certificate declines rather than lying. This separation-dependence is exactly
what the singular deflation of Section~\ref{sec:krylov} engineers for the far block.

\section{The certified Krylov method}\label{sec:krylov}

We now \emph{join} the two components: the Miyajima--Rump enclosure of Section~\ref{sec:mr} on a small
captured block, and the certified Lanczos floor of Section~\ref{sec:lanczos} on a matrix-free far
block. Now $A$ is large or matrix-free. The dense VBD of Section~\ref{sec:mr} cannot run on all of $A$,
but it can run on a small subspace. We split $\C^n$ into a captured subspace $\mathcal Q=\operatorname{ran}Q$
($Q$ with orthonormal columns) and its complement, and write $A$ in the block form of the orthonormal
basis $[\,Q\mid Q_\perp\,]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2block}
  A \;\cong\; \begin{pmatrix} G_{11} & G_{12}\\ G_{21} & G_{22}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  G_{11}=Q^*AQ,\quad G_{22}=P_\perp A P_\perp,\quad P_\perp=I-QQ^*.
\end{equation}
The off-diagonal blocks are the coupling; put $\gamma=\max(\norm{G_{12}}_2,\norm{G_{21}}_2)$. Because
the basis is orthonormal the outer similarity is unitary, so $\gamma=\norm{E}_2$ with
$E=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0&G_{12}\\G_{21}&0\end{smallmatrix}\big)$, and no $\kappa_2$ enters the
outer split. A Schur-complement bound gives, for $\sig_{11}=\sigmin(G_{11}-zI)$ and
$\sig_{22}=\sigmin(G_{22}-zI)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:assembly}
  \boxed{\;\sigmin(A-zI)\;\ge\;\frac{\sig_{11}\,\sig_{22}}{\sig_{11}+\sig_{22}+\gamma}\;}
\end{equation}
positive whenever $\sig_{11},\sig_{22}>0$, for \emph{any} coupling $\gamma$. (The cruder Weyl bound
$\min(\sig_{11},\sig_{22})-\gamma$ dies once $\gamma$ is large, which it is for non-normal operators.)

\paragraph{Block-Ger\v sgorin sharpening.} The assembly \eqref{eq:assembly} charges both blocks the
\emph{same} symmetric coupling $\gamma$. A block analogue of Feingold--Varga localizes it, charging
each block only its \emph{own} off-block row: $r_1=\norm{G_{12}}_2$ for the captured block, $r_2=\norm{G_{21}}_2$
for the far block. Because the outer basis is unitary this floor is $\kappa_2$-free.

\begin{proposition}[Outer block-dominance floor]\label{prop:outerbdom}
With $\sig_{11},\sig_{22}>0$ and $\tau(z)^2=\big(\norm{G_{12}}_2/\sig_{11}\big)^2+\big(\norm{G_{21}}_2/\sig_{22}\big)^2$,
\[
  \tau(z)<1\ \Longrightarrow\ \sigmin(A-zI)\;\ge\;\min(\sig_{11},\sig_{22})\,\big(1-\tau(z)\big).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Factor $A-zI=D\big(I+D^{-1}E\big)$ with $D=\operatorname{blockdiag}(G_{11}-zI,G_{22}-zI)$ and $E$ the
off-diagonal part. The block rows of $G=D^{-1}E$ are $(G_{11}-zI)^{-1}[\,0\ G_{12}\,]$ and
$(G_{22}-zI)^{-1}[\,G_{21}\ 0\,]$, of norms $\le\norm{G_{12}}_2/\sig_{11}$ and $\le\norm{G_{21}}_2/\sig_{22}$,
so $\norm{G}_2^2\le\sum_j\norm{G_{\text{row }j}}_2^2=\tau^2$. Then $\sigmin(I+G)\ge1-\tau$ and
$\sigmin(A-zI)\ge\sigmin(D)\,\sigmin(I+G)=\min(\sig_{11},\sig_{22})(1-\tau)$. This is the two-block
instance of the block-Ger\v sgorin/dominance floor for the Miyajima--Rump resolvent (the same argument
as the localized floor \eqref{eq:blockdom} of Section~\ref{sec:mrres}, here with $\kappa_2=1$).
\end{proof}

Both \eqref{eq:assembly} and Proposition~\ref{prop:outerbdom} are certified lower bounds on the same
$\sigmin(A-zI)$, so the engine returns their \emph{maximum}. The two are complementary: the
block-dominance floor is up to $\approx2\times$ tighter when the couplings are small relative to the
gaps --- the far field, away from the spectral cloud --- but it \emph{dies} ($\tau\ge1$) as $z$
approaches an eigenvalue, where $\sig_{11}\to0$; there the Schur-complement assembly, positive for any
$\gamma$, is the survivor. On the tight isolation circles of Section~\ref{sec:enclose}
($\sig_{11}\sim10^{-2}$, $\gamma\sim1$) only the assembly contributes; the sharpening acts on the
far-field resolvent (Figure~\ref{fig:farfield}), where it tightens the certified $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum
outside the far circle.

\begin{remark}[One Schur-complement thread]\label{rem:schur}
The three resolvent floors of this paper are the same idea at three scales. The Miyajima--Rump block
floor (Lemma~\ref{lem:blockres}) factors $M-zI=(\Lambda-zI)\big(I+(\Lambda-zI)^{-1}(M-\Lambda)\big)$;
the localized version \eqref{eq:blockdom} and the outer block-dominance
(Proposition~\ref{prop:outerbdom}) are the block-row (Schur-complement) refinements of that
factorization, charging each block only its own coupling; and the assembly \eqref{eq:assembly} is the
$2\times2$ Schur-complement bound of the captured/far split. In every case the object being bounded is
$\sigmin$ of a block after eliminating the coupling to the rest --- a Schur complement. The certified
Lanczos of Section~\ref{sec:lanczos} enters this thread as the \emph{matrix-free evaluation of the far
factor}: it floors $\sigmin(G_{22}-zI)$, the trailing block whose Schur complement completes the
$2\times2$ bound, without ever forming $G_{22}$. The Lanczos is thus not a separate device bolted on;
it is how one term of the Schur-complement assembly is certified when the operator is only a matvec.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Two subspaces, two jobs}\label{sec:twosub}

The captured subspace must do two things, and for a non-normal operator these require \emph{different}
directions.

\paragraph{Eigenvalues need the eigenvector (Arnoldi) block.} A few steps of Arnoldi reduce $A$ to a
dense upper Hessenberg Rayleigh block $H$ (the non-symmetry of $A$ enters here as a Hessenberg, not a
tridiagonal, reduction), whose Ritz values approximate the near-target eigenvalues. Running the VBD of
Section~\ref{sec:mr} on $H$ certifies the eigenvalue discs. Let $V$ ($n\times m_{\mathrm{eig}}$,
orthonormal) be the Arnoldi basis.

\paragraph{The resolvent tail needs the singular block.} The far-block operator norm
$\norm{G_{22}}_2=\norm{P_\perp A P_\perp}_2$ is a \emph{singular-value} quantity. The Arnoldi space is
$A$-nearly-invariant (the forward residual $\beta_N$ is tiny) but \emph{not} $A^*$-invariant, and its
own leading singular value falls short of $\sigmax(A)$; deflating it leaves $\norm{G_{22}}_2$ far above
$\sigma_{m+1}(A)$. Rotating inside $\operatorname{ran}V$ cannot help --- the singular vectors of the
compression $H$, lifted through the frame, give the \emph{same} projector $VV^*$. What is needed are
the top singular vectors of $A$ \emph{themselves}, which lie partly outside $\operatorname{ran}V$.
These are produced by a Lanczos on the Hermitian $A^*A$ (two matvecs per step): its Krylov space
$\{v,\,A^*Av,\,(A^*A)^2v,\dots\}$ converges to the top right singular vectors $U_k$, and by
Eckart--Young $\norm{(I-U_kU_k^*)A(I-U_kU_k^*)}_2\to\sigma_{k+1}$.

\begin{definition}[Captured subspace]\label{def:Q}
$Q=\operatorname{orth}[\,V\mid U'\,]$, where $U'=(I-VV^*)U_k$ is the part of the singular block outside
the eigenvector block (rank-revealing, drop near-zero columns). The eigenvalue enclosure reads its
discs from the VBD on $V^*AV$; the resolvent tail uses the enlarged $Q$.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Deep far block, and a stopping rule.} On the target circle the shift enters the far block
only diagonally, $P_\perp(A-zI)P_\perp=G_{22}-zI$, so by the reverse triangle inequality
\begin{equation}\label{eq:farshort}
  \sigmin(G_{22}-zI)\;\ge\;\abs{z}-\norm{G_{22}}_2,
\end{equation}
\emph{exact for every $z$}, with the single $z$-independent number $\norm{G_{22}}_2$. This is useful
only when the far block is deep. Fix a target circle of radius $R$; grow the singular block $U_k$ until
$\norm{G_{22}}_2<f\,R$ (we use $f=0.9$: the far circle need only sit inside $R$). Equivalently, peel
singular vectors until the smallest converged singular Ritz value drops below the scale of interest ---
detected robustly by the \emph{plateau} of the count $\#\{\sigma\ge\text{thresh}\}$, which the large
null space of $A^*A$ (numerical rank $\ll n$) cannot inflate. Every eigenvalue with $\abs{\lambda}>R$
is then captured and certifiable, automatically: no guess of ``how many'' is needed. The former
three-block picture (good/bad/ugly) collapses to \emph{captured-good $+$ deep-far}: the ``bad''
near-circle eigenvalues no longer need a separate block, because the deep far block absorbs them.

Writing $\Lambda_\varepsilon(M)=\{z:\sigmin(M-zI)\le\varepsilon\}$ for the $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum,
the assembly delivers something considerably stronger than a statement about eigenvalues: outside $R$,
the \emph{pseudospectrum} of $A$ is trapped inside a pseudospectrum of the small captured block.

\begin{proposition}[Pseudospectral inclusion outside $R$]\label{prop:complete}
Fix $R$ with $\norm{G_{22}}_2<R$ and put $\sig_{22}^-:=R-\norm{G_{22}}_2>0$. For $\varepsilon\ge0$ set
\[
  \delta(\varepsilon)\;:=\;\frac{\varepsilon\,\sig_{22}^-}{\varepsilon+\sig_{22}^-+\gamma}\;.
\]
Then
\[
  \Lambda_{\delta(\varepsilon)}(A)\cap\{\abs{z}>R\}\;\subseteq\;\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(G_{11})\cap\{\abs{z}>R\}.
\]
In particular, taking $\varepsilon=0$, $\ \spec(A)\cap\{\abs{z}>R\}\subseteq\spec(G_{11})\cap\{\abs{z}>R\}$:
if the verified block diagonalization of $G_{11}$ finds exactly $k$ eigenvalues of modulus $>R$, then
$A$ has \emph{no others} outside $R$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\abs{z}>R$ with $z\notin\Lambda_\varepsilon(G_{11})$, i.e.\ $\sig_{11}=\sigmin(G_{11}-zI)>\varepsilon$.
By \eqref{eq:farshort}, $\sig_{22}\ge\abs{z}-\norm{G_{22}}_2>\sig_{22}^-$. The right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:assembly} is increasing in each of $\sig_{11},\sig_{22}$, so
$\sigmin(A-zI)\ge\sig_{11}\sig_{22}/(\sig_{11}+\sig_{22}+\gamma)>\delta(\varepsilon)$, i.e.\
$z\notin\Lambda_{\delta(\varepsilon)}(A)$. The contrapositive is the claim; $\varepsilon=0$ gives the
spectral statement.
\end{proof}

The inclusion is only half of the story; a homotopy upgrades it to an exact \emph{count}.

\begin{proposition}[Exact exterior count]\label{prop:count}
Assume $\norm{G_{22}}_2<R$ and that the assembly floor is positive on the circle $\abs{z}=R$, i.e.
$\sigmin(G_{11}-zI)>0$ there. Then $A$ and $G_{11}$ have the \emph{same number} of eigenvalues, counted
with algebraic multiplicity, in $\{\abs{z}>R\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $t\in[0,1]$ set $A_t=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}G_{11}&tG_{12}\\ tG_{21}&G_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\big)$,
so $A_1=A$ and $A_0=G_{11}\oplus G_{22}$. The coupling of $A_t$ is $t\gamma\le\gamma$, and the
right-hand side of \eqref{eq:assembly} is decreasing in the coupling, so for every $t$ and every $z$
with $\abs{z}=R$,
\[
  \sigmin(A_t-zI)\;\ge\;\frac{\sig_{11}\sig_{22}}{\sig_{11}+\sig_{22}+t\gamma}
  \;\ge\;\frac{\sig_{11}\sig_{22}}{\sig_{11}+\sig_{22}+\gamma}\;>\;0,
\]
using $\sig_{22}\ge R-\norm{G_{22}}_2>0$ and the hypothesis $\sig_{11}>0$. Thus $A_t-zI$ is
nonsingular on the circle for all $t$: no eigenvalue of $A_t$ meets $\abs{z}=R$. Since the eigenvalues
of $A_t$ depend continuously on $t$ (they are the roots of a polynomial whose coefficients are
polynomial in $t$), the number lying in $\{\abs{z}>R\}$ is a continuous integer-valued function of
$t$, hence constant. At $t=0$ that number is
$\#\big(\spec(G_{11})\cap\{\abs{z}>R\}\big)+\#\big(\spec(G_{22})\cap\{\abs{z}>R\}\big)$, and the second
term vanishes because $\spec(G_{22})\subseteq\{\abs{z}\le\norm{G_{22}}_2\}$ with $\norm{G_{22}}_2<R$.
Evaluating at $t=1$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Certified spectral gap]\label{cor:gap}
Under the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:count}, suppose $G_{11}$ has exactly one eigenvalue
$\lambda_1$ of modulus $>R$. Then $A$ has exactly one such eigenvalue, and \emph{every} other
eigenvalue of $A$ satisfies $\abs{\lambda}\le R$. If $A$ is a stochastic matrix, so that
$\lambda_1=1$ is the Perron eigenvalue, the spectral gap obeys
\[
  1-\abs{\lambda_2}\;\ge\;1-R ,
\]
and the asymptotic decay of $\norm{A^k-\Pi}$ is governed by a rate no worse than $R$.
\end{corollary}

This is one of the more useful things the method does, and it applies well beyond transfer operators:
\emph{the spectral gap of a huge finite-state Markov chain}. A transition matrix of a chain on
millions of states is exactly the regime the method is built for --- large, sparse or matrix-free,
typically \emph{non-reversible} and hence non-normal, so the symmetric machinery of reversible chains
does not apply and Ritz values cannot be trusted. Corollary~\ref{cor:gap} converts a single certified
inequality $\norm{G_{22}}_2<R$ into a rigorous mixing-rate bound: certify that nothing but the Perron
eigenvalue lies outside $R$, and the chain provably mixes at rate $R$. Nothing about reversibility,
positivity or a spectral decomposition is used; the chain need only be applied to a vector, together
with its adjoint. We regard this, rather than any single spectral computation, as the practical point
of the construction.

It is worth isolating how little the two propositions actually ask for. Their entire hypothesis is the
single scalar inequality
\begin{equation}\label{eq:onehypothesis}
  \norm{G_{22}}_2\;<\;R ,
\end{equation}
certified once and $z$-independently. Everything else --- the inclusion, the count, the enclosures ---
follows. So the method reduces the exterior spectral problem for a large matrix-free non-normal
operator to \emph{certifying one number}: an upper bound on the norm of the deflated far block. If that
number comes in below $R$, the captured block has provably captured everything of interest outside $R$;
if it does not, the method declines. This is the whole content of the design, and it is why the
singular deflation of Section~\ref{sec:twosub} --- which is what makes $\norm{G_{22}}_2$ small --- is
the algorithmic heart of the method rather than an implementation detail.

Combined with Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} this settles the exterior completely: not only is every
eigenvalue of $A$ outside $R$ an eigenvalue of $G_{11}$, but the two exteriors carry the \emph{same
count}. In particular no eigenvalue of $G_{11}$ outside $R$ is a projection artifact --- the captured
subspace mixes near-invariant Arnoldi directions with $A^*A$-Lanczos directions chosen only to deepen
the far block, and one might worry that compressing onto the latter manufactures spurious eigenvalues,
but the count shows that whatever such artifacts exist must lie in the bulk $\abs{z}<R$. Applying the
same homotopy to any smaller contour on which the floor is positive transfers the count there too, so
each certified circle of Section~\ref{sec:enclose} encloses exactly as many eigenvalues of $A$ as of
$G_{11}$.

This is what the outside-$R$ construction is \emph{for}. The assembly \eqref{eq:assembly} is an
inequality valid at \emph{every} $z$ simultaneously, so once the deflation has made the far block deep
enough that $\sig_{22}>0$ on the whole exterior, the captured block \emph{controls the entire exterior}
--- not merely the eigenvalues, but every pseudospectral level set. No grid, no covering argument and
no a-priori bound on the spectral radius is required; the exterior is disposed of in one stroke, and
the quantitative version is exactly the rigorous pseudospectrum we plot in
Section~\ref{sec:examples}. Combined with the enclosures of Section~\ref{sec:enclose}, the output is a
\emph{complete} certified description of $\spec(A)$ outside $R$: a finite list of discs provably
containing every eigenvalue of modulus exceeding $R$, and nothing else. The distinction matters in
practice, since $\sig_{11}$ is itself only known through a verified enclosure: what one certifies at
finite precision is the inclusion at the resolution $\varepsilon$ that the verified SVD of $G_{11}$
attains, and Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} says precisely what that resolution buys. Choosing $R$
larger costs eigenvalues but buys margin, since $\sig_{22}^-=R-\norm{G_{22}}_2$ grows; this is the
practical tuning knob of the method.

The inclusion is one-directional, and it is worth being exact about what that does and does not
assert. Nothing outside $R$ is \emph{missed}. It is not asserted that every eigenvalue of $G_{11}$
outside $R$ is an eigenvalue of $A$: the captured subspace mixes near-invariant Arnoldi directions
with $A^*A$-Lanczos directions included to deepen the far block, and compressing onto the latter can
in principle manufacture eigenvalues of $G_{11}$ that are artifacts of the projection. Should one wish
to confirm an individual candidate, the coupling supplies a $\kappa$-free converse: if
$G_{11}w=\lambda w$ with $\norm{w}=1$ and $q=Xw$, then $Aq-\lambda q=G_{21}w$, and since
$\sigmin(M)\le\norm{Mq}$ for unit $q$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eta}
  \sigmin(A-\lambda I)\;\le\;\norm{G_{21}w},
\end{equation}
placing $\lambda$ in that pseudospectral level set of $A$. In the transfer-operator examples of
Section~\ref{sec:examples} the question does not arise: the eigenvalues of $G_{11}$ outside $R$
coincide with the converged Arnoldi values, the projection artifacts falling well inside the bulk
$\abs{z}<R$.

\paragraph{Faithfulness --- a statement about sharpness, not about validity.} Two things must be kept
apart here. That $\sig_{11}=\svdbox(G_{11}-zI)$ is a rigorous enclosure of $\sigmin(G_{11}-zI)$, and is
$\kappa_2$-free, is a \emph{theorem} (the exact-SVD escape of Section~\ref{sec:mrres}); it holds for any
$Q$ whatsoever, and the ill-conditioning of the augmented block is irrelevant to it. Nothing in the
soundness of \eqref{eq:assembly} depends on how well $G_{11}$ represents $A$. What the choice of $Q$
governs is only how \emph{tight} the resulting floor is, and this is a quantitative matter properly
settled by measurement: with $Q$ from Definition~\ref{def:Q} the compression is a \emph{faithful}
stand-in for $A$ on the target circles, $\sigmin(Q^*AQ-zI)$ agreeing with $\sigmin(A-zI)$ to
$\approx3\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:examples}), with no spurious eigenvalue of $Q^*AQ$ near the circles. A
poor choice of $Q$ would cost accuracy, never correctness.

\section{Certification estimates for Krylov}\label{sec:krylovcert}

We now assemble \eqref{eq:assembly} rigorously.

\subsection{The matrix-free far block}\label{sec:farblock}

Fix $z$ and put $M_z=(G_{22}-zI)^*(G_{22}-zI)\succeq0$ on $\operatorname{ran}P_\perp$; then
$\sigmin(G_{22}-zI)=\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(M_z)}$, and $M_z$ is applied by two matvecs (on $A,A^*$) plus
the rank-$\dim Q$ projector $P_\perp$, with $G_{22}$ never formed. This is exactly the setting of the
certified Lanczos decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:lanczos}. The Krylov run started from a vector in
$\operatorname{ran}P_\perp$ stays there ($M_z$ maps that subspace to itself), so the relevant invariant
subspace has dimension $N=n-\dim\mathcal Q$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:lanczosfloor} applied to $M_z$
with $k=N$ gives
\[
  \sigmin(G_{22}-zI)\;\ge\;\sqrt{\max\!\big(0,\ \theta_{\min}^--\beta_{\mathrm L}\big)},
  \qquad \beta_{\mathrm L}=\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{1-\rho}},
\]
a rigorous floor from $N$ matvecs alone. It is here that the \emph{deep} far block pays off: deflating the
top singular directions (Definition~\ref{def:Q}) lifts $\lambda_{\min}(M_z)$ away from $0$, so the
bottom Ritz value does \emph{not} converge early, the divisor $\underline{\bar\beta_j}$ stays healthy,
$\norm{R_2}_2<1$ holds, and the floor is sound and tight --- exactly the well-separated-bottom regime
Proposition~\ref{prop:lanczosfloor} rewards. On a shallow (eigenvector-only) far block the bottom
cluster collapses $\norm{R_2}_2$ and the certificate declines. In practice, once the far block is deep
the $z$-independent shortcut \eqref{eq:farshort} suffices on the circles, and the per-$z$ Lanczos is a
fallback triggered only where $\abs{z}\le\norm{G_{22}}_2$. Note that the shortcut needs an \emph{upper}
bound on $\norm{G_{22}}_2$, i.e.\ on $\lambda_{\max}$ of the same operator --- which requires the same
completeness direction, since a missed \emph{large} eigenvalue is as invisible to a partial basis as a
missed small one. We therefore obtain it from the $\lambda_{\max}$ half of
Proposition~\ref{prop:lanczosfloor} at full basis. This is the one $O(N)$-matvec computation of the
method, and it is performed \emph{once per operator}, not per $z$: the resulting $\norm{G_{22}}_2$ is
$z$-independent and every circle reuses it.

\subsection{The coupling}\label{sec:coupling}

$\gamma=\max(\norm{P_\perp AQ}_2,\norm{P_\perp A^*Q}_2)$ is $z$-independent and computed once. For a
non-normal $A$ the two differ (the adjoint coupling dominates) and both are needed. The $n\times\dim Q$
coupling matrix is \emph{wide}, and the cheap $\min(\text{Collatz},\sqrt{\norm{\cdot}_1\norm{\cdot}_\infty})$
bound overestimates its spectral norm by $\approx\sqrt{\text{rank}}$ (measured $5.34$ vs.\ true $1.33$);
this alone can sink \eqref{eq:assembly}. We therefore bound it via the small $\dim Q\times\dim Q$ Gram,
$\gamma=\sqrt{\sigmax(C^*C)}$ with $C$ the coupling matrix, using a verified SVD of $C^*C$ --- tight and
cheap.

\subsection{Circle certificates: the adaptive pearl cover}\label{sec:pearl}

To certify $\sigmin(A-zI)>0$ over a whole circle $\Gamma=\{c+r e^{i\theta}\}$ we cover it with
\emph{pearls}. At a pearl centre $z$ a verified point SVD gives $\sig(z)=\sigmin(G_{11}-zI)$; since
$\sigmin(G_{11}-wI)$ is $1$-Lipschitz in $w$, the disc $\{\abs{w-z}\le\varrho\}$ has $\sigmin\ge\sig(z)-\varrho$, and we advance $\Delta\theta=2\varrho/r$ so the pearls overlap. The pearl radius
$\varrho=\eta\,\sig(z)$ adapts to the local $\sigmin$ --- large pearls where $\sigmin$ is large, small
where small --- so a smooth arc costs one SVD and the whole circle takes $O(\oint r\,d\theta/\sigmin)$
SVDs instead of a fixed uniform sampling with a global arc-cover. (Using an interval scalar
$\Ball(z,\varrho)$ on the diagonal is much looser: it perturbs each diagonal \emph{independently},
a far larger set than the common shift.) The far term contributes $\sig_{22}\ge\min_\Gamma\abs{z}
-\norm{G_{22}}_2$ (exact, analytic), and \eqref{eq:assembly} is monotone in both, so the rigorous
floor over $\Gamma$ is one assembly of the two circle minima.

\subsection{Rigorous eigenvalue enclosure: probe and triple}\label{sec:enclose}

A positive floor on a circle $\Gamma$ enclosing exactly one eigenvalue certifies that eigenvalue of
$A$ inside $\Gamma$; shrinking $\Gamma$ tightens the enclosure. Blind bisection is wasteful, so we use
a two-step scheme. \emph{(a)} A cheap non-rigorous probe: grow $r$ from the eigenvalue until the
Float64 $\sigmin(G_{11}-(c+r)I)$ matches the linear estimate $r/\kappa(\lambda)$ (it has cleared the
numerical noise floor), where $\kappa(\lambda)$ is the eigenvalue condition number read from the frame
and $r_{\mathrm{heur}}=\kappa(\lambda)\cdot\norm{\text{lifted Ritz residual}}$ (Bauer--Fike) sets the
starting scale. \emph{(b)} Triple the distance and run \emph{one} rigorous pearl cover there, growing
by a factor until $\svdbox$ certifies $\sigmin(A-zI)>0$. The rigorous radius is floored at the verified
$\svdbox$ noise level $\kappa(G_{11})\cdot u$, which is above the Float64 $\sigmin$ noise; a
higher-precision (BigFloat) SVD lowers it for a tighter enclosure. The heuristic only \emph{locates}
where to certify; the enclosure is rigorous.

\subsection{Soundness summary}\label{sec:sound}

Every quantity feeding \eqref{eq:assembly} is a ball bound: $\sig_{11}$ from a verified SVD of the
Float64 candidate block (the block entries carry only rounding, subsumed as in Section~\ref{sec:mr}),
$\sig_{22}$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:lanczosfloor} or the exact shortcut, and $\gamma$ from a verified
Gram SVD. The routine refuses when $\norm{R_2}_2\ge1$. The rigorous $\sigmin$ never exceeds
$\sigmin(A-zI)$; at a true eigenvalue the gap is $\le0$.

\section{Numerical examples}\label{sec:examples}

\subsection{Transfer operators of chaotic maps (matrix-free)}

We take $A$ the Ulam (Perron--Frobenius) discretization of a noisy map on a uniform grid, applied
matrix-free. Noise width $0.1$; depth-$6$ grid, $n=4096$.

\paragraph{Noisy H\'enon.} Strongly non-normal ($\sigmax(A)=1.71$ vs.\ spectral radius $1$), numerical
rank $\approx745$, spectrum a cloud inside the unit disk. The eigenvector Krylov complement has
$\norm{G_{22}}_2=0.43$ against $\sigma_{257}(A)=0.12$ --- the symptom (S2). Running the automatic
outside-$R$ mode at $R=0.6$, the singular deflation ($n_{\mathrm{sing}}=54$, $\dim\mathcal Q=134$)
certifies $\norm{G_{22}}_2=0.445<R$, discharging the single hypothesis
\eqref{eq:onehypothesis}; the far-block $\lambda_{\max}$ bound is the full-basis certificate of
Proposition~\ref{prop:lanczosfloor} at $N=n-\dim\mathcal Q=3962$ steps. Propositions~\ref{prop:complete}
and~\ref{prop:count} then give the exterior outright: $A$ has \textbf{exactly four eigenvalues of
modulus $>0.6$}, namely $\lambda=1.0000$, $0.4514\pm0.5290i$ and $-0.6748$, with certified floors
$\sigmin\ge1.04\!\times\!10^{-2}$, $1.10\!\times\!10^{-3}$ and $3.87\!\times\!10^{-4}$ on their
isolation circles and rigorous enclosures of radius $10^{-7}$ (the Float64 $\svdbox$ resolution).
Wall time $\approx290$\,s. The captured compression is faithful: $\sigmin(G_{11}-zI)=\sigmin(A-zI)$ to
$\approx3\%$ at the tested points.

\paragraph{Contracting Lorenz.} A sharper spectral gap: the Arnoldi Ritz moduli are
$0.9988,\,0.3151,\,0.3151,\,0.2690,\dots$, so a single eigenvalue lies outside $R=0.6$. With
$n_{\mathrm{sing}}=12$ and $\dim\mathcal Q=92$ the deflation certifies $\norm{G_{22}}_2=0.234<R$, and
the method returns \textbf{exactly one eigenvalue outside $0.6$}, $\lambda=0.9988$, with
$\sigmin\ge2.27\!\times\!10^{-2}$ and enclosure $10^{-7}$; wall time $\approx212$\,s. The
eigenvalue is not exactly $1$ because this discretization is not mass-preserving (the map is not
periodic on the domain, and we measure a $1.3\%$ defect in $A^*\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1$). Its resolvent
behaves as expected for a simple isolated eigenvalue: we measure $\sigmin(G_{11}-zI)\approx0.57\abs{z-\lambda}$
near it, the factor being the eigenvalue condition number $\kappa(\lambda)\approx1.8$.

\paragraph{Gauss--Kuzmin--Wirsing.} A benchmark whose sub-dominant eigenvalue (the GKW constant) is a
classical target; the far block is low-rank, and a small captured block certifies the gap~\cite{NisoliGauss}.

\subsection{Resolvent sharpening in the far field}\label{sec:farfielddemo}

The block-dominance floor of Proposition~\ref{prop:outerbdom} sharpens the certified resolvent exactly
where the couplings are small relative to the gaps. We exhibit it on a transfer-like operator --- a
Gaussian-smoothing convolution ($n=300$, row-stochastic, spectral radius $1$) whose singular values
decay to $10^{-8}$ by index $50$, so a captured block of dimension $\dim Q=52$ drives the far block to
$\norm{G_{22}}_2\approx0$ and the coupling to $\gamma\approx0$: the pure decoupling limit. Over a
grid of $19{,}880$ points outside the far circle, the block-dominance floor improves the pure
Schur-complement floor by a factor $f_{\mathrm{new}}/f_{\mathrm{old}}$ of \emph{median $1.99$, maximum
$2.00$} (above $1.05$ at $99\%$ of points) --- the theoretical $2\times$ ceiling of the sharpening
($\gamma\to0$ turns $\sig_{11}\sig_{22}/(\sig_{11}+\sig_{22})$ into $\tfrac12\min$, which block-dominance
lifts to $\min$). Figure~\ref{fig:farfield} overlays the two certified $\varepsilon$-pseudospectral
contours: the block-dominance contours (solid) lie strictly inside the Schur-only contours (dashed).
The gain is confined to the far field, as Proposition~\ref{prop:outerbdom} predicts; on the tight
isolation circles it vanishes and the assembly \eqref{eq:assembly} carries the certificate.

\subsection{Where the method applies --- and where it does not}\label{sec:testmat}

A word first on what these matrices are for. The classical non-normal gallery --- \textsc{Grcar},
\textsc{Kahan}, the Jordan block, convection--diffusion --- consists of \emph{small dense} matrices.
They are therefore tests of the \emph{first} component of this paper, the Miyajima--Rump enclosure of
Sections~\ref{sec:mr}--\ref{sec:mrres}, which is what one would actually run on them: there is no
reason to reduce a $100\times100$ dense matrix by a Krylov method, and the outer split has nothing to
do. What they probe is whether the dense enclosure behaves correctly on defective, near-defective and
strongly non-normal spectra --- whether the block region of Theorem~\ref{thm:enclosure} tracks
$\abs{z-\lambda}^m$ at a Jordan block, whether $\kappa_2$ stays benign on the block-orthonormal frame,
and whether the Neumann test refuses rather than lies when the eigenbasis is hopeless.

The Krylov layer is a different question with a different test set, and it is worth being explicit
that the gallery does \emph{not} test it. That layer rests on making $\norm{G_{22}}_2$ small by
singular deflation, which requires the singular values to \emph{decay} so the resolvent tail is
genuinely low-dimensional --- the transfer-operator and Markov-chain regime of
Section~\ref{sec:examples}. Applying it to a small full-rank matrix is a category error: the deflation
has nothing to remove, $\norm{G_{22}}_2$ stays comparable to the eigenvalue scale, hypothesis
\eqref{eq:onehypothesis} fails and the method declines. We record below what happens when one does so
anyway (Table~\ref{tab:gallery}), because the boundary of applicability is worth mapping honestly, but
the reader should read those rows as delimiting the \emph{Krylov reduction}, not the enclosure.

\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{bench/figures/regime_decay.png}
\caption{The method's regime, visualized: normalized singular-value decay $\sigma_k/\sigma_1$. A

\label{fig:regime}

\paragraph{Separation, where it is possible and where it is not.} The candidate similarity may be
built from the eigenvector matrix or from the Schur vectors, and neither dominates; the
implementation races the candidates and keeps the best certified one. The gallery shows both regimes.
For \textsc{Kahan} and the convection--diffusion operator the eigenbasis, though ill-conditioned
($\operatorname{cond}(V)\approx10^{13}$ and $10^{11}$), is still usable at working precision, and the
enclosure separates the spectrum completely: $60$ and $80$ \emph{singleton} clusters with radii below
$1.4\times10^{-8}$ and $1.7\times10^{-2}$. For \textsc{Grcar} the eigenvector basis is
\emph{exponentially} ill-conditioned in $n$ --- we measure
$\operatorname{cond}(V)=6.9\cdot10^{2},\,2.8\cdot10^{6},\,1.5\cdot10^{10},\,5.1\cdot10^{17}$ at
$n=20,40,60,100$ --- and at $n=100$ this exceeds $1/u\approx4.5\cdot10^{15}$, so no eigenvector frame
is admissible: the computed $V$ is singular to working precision and the Neumann test returns
$\norm{R_2}_\infty=54.6\ge1$. There the unitary Schur frame takes over, certifying with
$\norm{R_2}\approx10^{-14}$ and $\kappa_2=1$, at the price of returning a single connected region
rather than $100$ discs. That is the honest answer for a matrix whose eigenvalues have condition
number $10^{17}$: they are not separable, and the method declines to pretend otherwise rather than
splitting them.

\paragraph{Defective spectrum: the Jordan block.} A single Jordan block $J_\lambda$ has one
eigenvector and infinite eigenvalue condition number, and it behaves exactly as
Theorem~\ref{thm:enclosure} predicts: both frames return \emph{one cluster of size $n$}, never $n$
separate discs. This is the point of using $\sigmin$ of a block rather than an eigenvalue distance ---
the region $\{z:\sigmin(P-zI)\le\beta_\Lambda\}$ tracks $\abs{z-\lambda}^m$, and splitting it would be
the failure mode. The same happens on \textsc{Demmel}'s counterexample~\cite{Demmel1987}, and it is
worth following that case through, because it shows precisely which quantity controls the enclosure.
\textsc{Demmel}'s matrix is graded over $36$ orders of magnitude, $\norm{A}_2=10^{36}$. The Schur
frame is unitary, so $\norm{R_2}_2=4.4\times10^{-16}$ is at the rounding level and $\kappa_2=1$; and
with a single cluster the off-block part vanishes, $N=0$. Yet \eqref{eq:blockdev} then reads
\[
  \beta_\Lambda=\frac{\norm{R_2}_2\norm{\widetilde B}_2}{1-\norm{R_2}_2}
  \;\approx\;4.4\times10^{-16}\cdot10^{36}\;=\;4.4\times10^{20},
\]
and the certified region has radius $\approx10^{36}$ --- as large as $\norm{A}$ itself. The enclosure
is perfectly \emph{sound} and entirely \emph{vacuous}. The lesson is that $\beta_\Lambda$ scales with
$\norm{\widetilde B}$, so what the residual layer delivers is a bound \emph{relative} to the norm of
the matrix; on a spectrum graded across $36$ decades it carries no information about the eigenvalues.

Two things rescue it, and they are worth separating. First, the \emph{exact-SVD escape} of
Section~\ref{sec:mrres} does not pay $\norm{\widetilde B}$ at all: taking
$\sigmin(A-zI)$ from a verified $\svdbox$ rather than from a residual improves the localization from
radius $10^{36}$ to $10^{30}$. Second --- and this is the real obstruction --- a verified SVD in
working precision has an \emph{absolute} noise floor $\approx u\norm{A}=10^{-16}\cdot10^{36}=10^{20}$,
so no Float64 computation can resolve an eigenvalue at $-1$. The impediment is precision, not
structure. Since the whole pipeline is generic in the float type, raising it settles the matter: at
$256$ bits the eigenvalue is enclosed in a disc of radius $1$, and at $512$ bits in a disc of radius
$\boldsymbol{10^{-2}}$, with certified values
\[
  \sigmin(A-zI)\;\ge\;3.9\cdot10^{-39},\quad 4.7\cdot10^{-47},\quad 9.2\cdot10^{-57}
  \qquad\text{at}\qquad \abs{z-\lambda}=1,\;10^{-1},\;10^{-2}.
\]
Those three numbers fall by ten orders per decade of $\abs{z-\lambda}$: this is exactly the
$\abs{z-\lambda}^{m}$ law of Theorem~\ref{thm:enclosure} for the single Jordan block of size $m=10$,
observed rather than assumed, and it is why the block region --- and not an eigenvalue distance --- is
the right object.

We note also one implementation caveat, distinct from the above. When $\operatorname{cond}(V)$
overflows (it reaches $10^{303}$ for the Jordan block and $10^{177}$ for \textsc{Demmel}) the computed
$\kappa_2$ came out \texttt{NaN}. Theorem~\ref{thm:enclosure} does not involve $\kappa_2$ --- it uses
$\sigmin(P_j-zI)$ and $\beta_\Lambda$, the latter through $\norm{R_2}$ as just displayed --- so the
enclosure itself was never affected; but the resolvent transport of Section~\ref{sec:mrres} divides
by $\kappa_2$, where a \texttt{NaN} would silently pass every comparison. The implementation now
returns $\infty$ instead, so that floor is vacuous rather than meaningless.

\paragraph{On the Krylov reduction.} For completeness we record what the reduction of
Section~\ref{sec:krylov} does on these matrices, remembering that they are not its intended input.
Their singular values do not decay, so the deflation has nothing to remove and
\eqref{eq:onehypothesis} fails: on \textsc{Grcar} $\norm{G_{22}}=2.0$ against $\abs{\lambda}=2.3$, on
the convection--diffusion operator $491$ against $800$, and on the unitary cyclic shift (all
$\sigma=1$) $\norm{G_{22}}=1$ identically, so the far block can never be made deep. In each case the
assembly returns a non-positive floor and the method declines. For small $n$ the captured frame can
even exhaust $\C^n$, leaving no far block at all. These are correct negatives; the regime is
Figure~\ref{fig:regime}.

We include the cyclic shift also because it is the textbook Arnoldi-failure case: from a coordinate
start, restarted Arnoldi with exact shifts can deflate the wanted eigenvector~\cite{Embree2009}.
\emph{We note honestly that our own random-start, fully-reorthogonalized Arnoldi does \textbf{not}
reproduce the collapse} (its Ritz moduli come out $\approx0.99$, near the true unit circle); the
classic pathology is a coordinate-start/restart phenomenon. The method's guarantee is not that Arnoldi
is trustworthy but that the certificate is.

\paragraph{Real-application sparse matrices (Matrix Market).} The gallery above is a set of
constructed pathologies. The proper test of the Krylov layer is sparse operators that arise from
applications, and we take them from the \textsc{Non-Hermitian Eigenvalue Problem}
collection~\cite{MatrixMarket}: the Brusselator reaction--diffusion family \textsc{rdb} and
\textsc{Olmstead}. (\textsc{Tolosa} is listed in that collection but is not served at any path we
could reach.) Here $R=\sqrt{\abs{\lambda_1}\abs{\lambda_2}}$, so a successful run certifies a
spectral gap. Table~\ref{tab:mm} reports the outcome; three of the four certify, and the fourth
declines.

The instructive part is \emph{which} one declines, because it sharpens the regime statement. We have
been saying that the method needs the singular values to decay, but \textsc{rdb200} certifies with
$\sigma_{50}/\sigma_1=0.59$ --- barely any decay at all --- while \textsc{olm1000} fails with a
comparable profile. The quantity that actually decides is $\norm{G_{22}}_2$ against $R$, and what
governs \emph{that} is the departure from normality measured by $\norm{A}_2/\rho(A)$. For the
\textsc{rdb} matrices this ratio is $\approx1.05$: the norm is essentially the spectral radius, a
modest deflation suffices, and $\dim\mathcal Q$ stays small ($93$ of $200$, $127$ of $800$). For
\textsc{Olmstead} it is $\approx9$, and the deflation must work much harder --- \textsc{olm500} only
succeeds by capturing $279$ of $500$ dimensions, over half the space, which is brute force rather
than a favourable regime --- while at $n=1000$ it cannot close the gap at all and the hypothesis
\eqref{eq:onehypothesis} fails. That is the honest boundary: not ``the singular values decay'' in the
abstract, but $\norm{G_{22}}_2<R$ after a deflation one can afford.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The method certifies the leading spectrum and resolvent of large non-normal and matrix-free operators
by combining the Miyajima--Rump verified block diagonalization on a faithful captured block with a
matrix-free far block made \emph{deep} by singular-vector deflation. It never trusts a Ritz value:
the argument-principle count and the assembled $\sigmin$ floor are the arbiters.
